How Does This Person Know So Much About You?

Leaked personal data can make scams feel genuine. See how your information can be misused, and what to do before you act.

Before you act

Four habits can help when a request feels unexpected.

THINKPause before acting.
VERIFYUse a separate trusted channel.
PROTECTSecure accounts and information.
REPORTRaise suspicious activity promptly.

Five ways leaked data gets misused

Select a scenario to read the full story.

1Impersonating a friendStolen conversations are used to trick you into sending money.

A message arrives on WhatsApp from a familiar friend.

“It was great catching up over dinner last week. I have an urgent payment to make. Could you lend me some money?”

The name, profile picture and private details all seem right, so you transfer the money. If your friend’s account has been compromised and their conversations have been read, those details could be used to create a convincing request in their name.

Watch for

An unexpected or urgent request for money.

What to do

If someone asks for money, do not rely on the same chat to verify the request. Contact the person through another channel, such as a phone number you already know.

Real cases and official warnings. Germany’s Federal Office for Information Security (BSI) warns that criminals can abuse WhatsApp’s device-linking feature to read chats. Singapore Police have also reported cases in which compromised WhatsApp accounts were used to ask contacts for emergency loans and trick them into transferring money.[1][2]

2Fake hotel paymentYour real booking details make a fake payment request look legitimate.

Before your trip, a message arrives claiming to be from your hotel. The hotel name and dates are correct.

“To keep your reservation, please confirm your card details.”

Not wanting your booking to be cancelled, you follow the link and enter your card details. But the website is fake.

Watch for

A payment or card request sent through an unexpected message or link.

What to do

Verify the request through the hotel’s or booking service’s official contact channels, not through the link in the message. If you have entered card details on a suspicious website, contact your card issuer promptly.

Real cases and official warnings. The operator of the hotel reservation management system TEMAIRAZU has confirmed suspicious messages impersonating hotels and other booking-related services, requesting card details or payment to maintain reservations. Although the operator does not hold card information, recipients are being directed to enter their card details.[3]

3Fake police callYour personal details are used to gain trust and pressure you to pay.

A caller reads out your name and address.

“This is the police. Your bank account has been used in a crime.”

Because the caller knows your details, you believe the call is genuine. You transfer money to a specified account, supposedly to assist an investigation.

Watch for

A caller who uses authority, urgency or personal details to pressure you.

What to do

Japanese police do not contact people through LINE or instruct them to transfer money to an account in an individual’s name. End the call and verify it using a police station’s contact details that you have found independently.

Real cases and official warnings. The National Consumer Affairs Center of Japan (NCAC), a public consumer-protection body, warns against trusting callers simply because they know your name or address. It has reported a case in which a person transferred JPY 1.5 million to someone posing as the police, although no link to a specific cyberattack has been established.[4]

4Password reuseA leaked password from one service can open the door to other accounts.

Your login ID and password are leaked from an online shopping site you used in the past.

“I do not use that site anymore.”

You take no action. But someone uses the same password to access another shopping account, where purchases are made without your knowledge.

Watch for

The same or similar password used across multiple services.

What to do

Use a different password for each service. Enable multi-factor authentication or use passkeys where supported.

Real cases and official warnings. IPA, a Japanese public agency that promotes information security and other IT initiatives, explains how leaked login IDs and passwords can be used to access other services. It also describes reports of unauthorised purchases and posts or solicitations sent from compromised social media accounts.[5]

5Fake offer to helpScammers pretend to help after a data leak, then steal more of your personal information.

The day after you see news of a data leak, someone calls claiming to represent a consumer advice organisation.

“Your information has also been leaked. Please provide your identity details so we can remove it.”

Wanting reassurance, you hand over more personal information. An attempt to protect yourself becomes the starting point for another scam.

Watch for

An unsolicited offer to remove leaked information that asks for more personal details.

What to do

NCAC does not call people offering to delete their personal information. Do not comply with suspicious requests. Verify them through official contact channels.

Real cases and official warnings. NCAC warns about calls made in its name offering to remove leaked personal information. It has also reported cases in which callers used supposed removal procedures to obtain a person’s My Number, Japan’s individual identification number.[6][7]

They know about you. That does not mean you should trust them.

If someone asks for money or personal information, pause and verify through a separate channel.
