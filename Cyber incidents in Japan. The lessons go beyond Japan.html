Japan and wider regional context

The impact is not confined to Japan.

Incidents span multiple regionsJapan, South Korea, Denmark, Bangladesh and Nepal

Incidents are being reported across industries in Japan, prompting the Information-technology Promotion Agency (IPA) to call for prompt security checks.[4] But the impact is not confined to Japan.

Data theft and operational disruption have also been reported across South Korea, Denmark, Bangladesh and Nepal. These incidents span multiple regions worldwide.[5][6][7]

AI misuse is also being reportedThe fundamentals stay the same

CrowdStrike reported the use of AI agents and other tools in attacks targeting South Korean financial institutions.[5] The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre also assesses that AI will increase the efficiency of cyber operations.[8]

Regardless of whether AI is involved, the fundamentals remain the same: reduce exposure, strengthen access controls and be ready to respond.
Cyber incidents across multiple regions highlight risks in both our own systems and external dependencies. The key question is: what can we learn and strengthen now?

Four lessons to act on

Each one comes with questions to ask your own team.

01Reduce opportunities for intrusionInternet-facing assets

skyticket reported that misuse of a management function enabled access to other servers and cloud-held data.[9] DIP’s incident involved a flaw in a website function.[2] Checks must go beyond patching to include application functionality and configuration.

ASK YOURSELF

  • Do we know all internet-facing websites, APIs and legacy functions?
  • Are vulnerabilities, configurations and access controls regularly tested?
  • Have unnecessary exposures been removed?
Check the full exposed estate, not just the main systems.
02Limit the impact of compromised accessAccess control

In Denmark’s CPR incident, a company’s legitimate lookup access was abused.[6] Alongside protecting entry points, design access so compromised permissions cannot expose more information than necessary.

ASK YOURSELF

  • Have we removed unnecessary accounts and permissions, and limited access to required data?
  • Can we detect bulk extraction and revoke sessions and integration keys?
Successful login does not mean safe access.
03Know where our dependencies create exposureThird-party risk

IDC Frontier’s attack affected many customers.[3] The disclosure alone does not establish a failure in vendor assessment, but it illustrates how an incident at a dependency can affect our operations.

ASK YOURSELF

  • Do we know what data and access our providers hold?
  • Are security controls verified with evidence?
  • Are incident notification, emergency contacts and responsibilities clear?
  • Have we tested contingency and recovery arrangements together?
Verify both routine assurance and the ability to respond together.
04Be ready before an incident happensIncident response

A provider reports suspected unauthorised access, but the full impact is still unknown.

Who receives the report? Who decides whether to restrict or stop the service? Who acts if the decision-maker is unavailable?

Do not wait for perfect information. Follow the established reporting process and be ready to investigate, contain and make decisions in parallel.

Takeaway: controls must work in practice

For us and our providers, having controls in place is only the starting point.

VerifyTest key controls and retain evidence that they work.
Close gapsAssign clear owners and deadlines to unresolved issues.
Learn and actTurn lessons from other organisations’ incidents into action before they become our own.
